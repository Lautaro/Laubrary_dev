## What was wrong

**The editor could be closed by a hard crash whenever the sound library was invalidated while something was playing.**
That is not an unusual situation — it is what happens every time a sound is edited while it can be heard, which is the
normal way this tool is used.

The engine's teardown asked the audio graph to destroy a playing sound, but nothing told the AUDIO SOURCE carrying that
sound to stop. An audio source that is still playing keeps asking for audio every block, so it went on asking a sound
that no longer existed. The engine complained about an invalid handle once per audio block — about seven complaints every
twenty milliseconds — and after twenty-seven seconds of that its own thread-safety validation faulted and took the
editor down with it.

## How it was established, rather than argued

The crash was read out of the engine's own log for the crashed session, not inferred. Three things in it settle the case:

- The complaints begin at the exact moment the teardown ran, and not a moment before.
- They repeat at precisely the audio block rate, for twenty-seven seconds, roughly fifteen thousand lines of them.
- The fault itself lands inside the engine's realtime-access validation — its own guard against exactly this, rather
  than a stray pointer somewhere in our own audio code.

**A predecessor's hypothesis was that this was memory being freed under the audio thread by the work in progress. That
was wrong, and worth recording as wrong.** The fault is in the engine's validation of a destroyed processor, and the
condition that produces it lives in code that predates this task entirely: it fires on the ordinary library-invalidation
path with no new work involved. The in-progress work did not cause it; it exercised it and made it visible.

## The fix

The order of a teardown is now the reverse of the obvious one, and each step is there for a reason that was observed:

1. **Silence what is pulling on the sound first.** Destroying a sound does not stop its audio source, and an audio source
   that is still playing will either keep asking the corpse for audio or start a fresh sound behind you.
2. **Then wait until the sound has been SEEN to stop being rendered** — silencing is a request like any other, and takes
   effect a block later.
3. **Only then destroy it.** A destroy issued while a block is in flight is precisely what crashed.
4. **Honour a refusal.** When a sound cannot be seen to stop, it is deliberately left playing and said so out loud.
   That wastes a voice and makes an edit appear not to have taken effect until the sound ends. Destroying it instead
   risks the editor, so this is not a close call.

Tearing everything down does the three phases across all sounds at once rather than one sound at a time, so the wait is
paid once — twenty sounds playing costs one settle window, not twenty.

There is deliberately **no remaining way to destroy a sound without going through this sequence.** The unsafe version was
not kept behind a flag, because it is the crash.

## How "has it stopped" is answered

Each playing sound bumps a counter on its way into and out of every block. Odd means a block is running, even means none
is. Quiet is the counter being even AND unchanged across a settle window of a little over two audio blocks — long enough
that another block would have had to begin within it if any were still coming.

Two details that make it trustworthy rather than merely plausible:

- **The counter does not belong to the sound.** It is allocated by the host and outlives the sound on purpose, because
  every other piece of the sound's bookkeeping lives in the memory whose safety is the question being asked.
- **One counter per playing sound, not one per audio source.** The first version shared one across every sound an audio
  source ever played, and that version produced a reading indistinguishable between "the sound we just tore down is
  still going" and "a different sound started". A barrier that cannot tell those apart is not a barrier — and this is a
  fourth entry for the list of times reasoning about this engine was wrong until something was measured.

The waiting sleeps in slices rather than spinning, and **that the audio side genuinely keeps going while the main thread
sleeps was measured, not assumed** — otherwise every "it went quiet" reading would have been the waiting's own doing.

## Verified

- **By probe:** a clean compile of the whole project, zero errors. Check 14 step 2 measured three blocks rendered during
  a sixty-millisecond sleep of the main thread, matching the expected rate exactly, which is what makes a sleeping
  barrier a valid observation. Check 14 step 3, after the fix: the sound was seen to stop before being destroyed, the
  whole teardown took fifty-two milliseconds against a forty-seven millisecond settle window (so one wait, not one per
  sound), zero blocks rendered after it returned, the audio source stopped, nothing left holding the sound, and **zero**
  engine complaints where the crashing version produced about fifteen thousand lines of them.
- **By eye:** nothing. There is nothing visual in this.
- **Not verified:** that the crash cannot happen by some other route. What was fixed is the route that was observed
  crashing, on the engine's own invalidation path. Long-run soak behaviour under heavy overlapping playback has not been
  exercised.

## Left open, deliberately

Immediately after a confirmed single-sound teardown, the count of sounds believed to be playing read one rather than
zero. The load-bearing readings around it were all correct (nothing was left holding the sound, nothing rendered
afterwards), and a stale entry is self-healing on the next pass — but it is unexplained and the leading suspicion is
that the graph asks for an instance more than once per play and the earlier handle is lost. Worth a look; not a blocker,
and not a memory-safety matter.
