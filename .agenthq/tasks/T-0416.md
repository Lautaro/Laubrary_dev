## What the owner reported

That the analyser had controls which had to be adjusted before the picture could be believed, and that the display was
too coarse. Their standard, which is the right one: a measuring tool shows what is actually happening without anybody
tuning it first, and it always shows the whole picture.

## What was wrong

**The vertical scale was fixed at twelve decibels.** Almost any real chain drove most bands hard against the top of the
graph, so a filter that cut a band by fifteen decibels and one that annihilated it by sixty looked exactly alike. The two
controls beside it — one toggling extra information, one asking for a fresh measurement — put the burden of getting an
accurate picture on the reader.

## What it is now

The scale is **fitted to whatever was measured**, rounded to a tidy step, with a floor so that a chain doing almost
nothing is not magnified into looking dramatic. The number it was fitted to is printed under the graph, so the scale is
never a mystery. Both controls are gone: the extra information is always shown because it is information rather than
decoration, and it re-measures itself whenever the chain changes, which is the only time the answer can differ.
Resolution went from twenty-four bands to **seventy-two**, and all seventy-two are measurable.

## The part that nearly went wrong, and how it was caught

Raising the detail brought back the flicker that had just been eliminated — quietly, in a way that looked fine.

Finer bands need a longer look at the signal to tell frequencies apart, which would mean fewer readings over time and a
display that barely moves. The apparent way out was to slide the long window along in small steps, and the reasoning
seemed airtight: the test signal repeats, so a window taken part way along holds the same material merely started at a
different point, and starting point does not change how much of each frequency is present.

**That reasoning was wrong**, because the window is not a plain cut: the samples are faded up and down across it to stop
its own edges registering. Material that has slid along sits under a different part of that fade and comes out slightly
different — and where an effect has cut a band almost to nothing, a slight change in a tiny number is a large change in
the ratio the display shows.

It was caught by re-running the same sixteen-effect sweep used to verify the original fix, which reported a waveshaper —
an effect with no memory at all, and therefore physically incapable of varying over time — as moving by five and a half
decibels. Moving the window by exactly one whole repeat of the test signal removes it completely, at the honest price of
readings arriving one repeat apart.

## Verified

Every effect that should hold still reads **0.0** movement at the new resolution, including the waveshaper that exposed
the regression; every effect that genuinely sweeps keeps its full movement (around eighteen to twenty-one decibels for
the three modulated ones). The depth of each effect's real influence is unchanged, so nothing was smoothed away.

The layout-stability check also caught a fresh breach introduced while doing this: the text replacing the removed
controls wrapped onto a second line in a narrow panel and grew the toolbar by a row. Fixed, and all twelve layout states
match again.
