## Description

Owner's original idea: a Kiln-style project produces sci-fi "tech marking" surface detail -- circuit-board-like etchings, plates with rings of dots and ridges. If Shaper/Tapestry can procedurally generate that kind of material as a height/bevel field, could the same field carve real relief into a 3D mesh, not just paint a 2D height buffer? "That would be very powerful."

**No exact Kiln source project was found for this specific look.** The available Kiln projects (`D:\CODEZ\Kiln\projects\`: Cloud, Directional Blast, Energy Explosion, Energy Projectile, Flame, Pyre Edge/Edge Dissolve/Fill/Fill Dissolve/Wipe, Sigil, Tapestry Shape, Tapestry Surface) were checked -- Sigil is a 2D combat-FX sprite pack (rings, spikes, flow), not a surface-marking generator, and nothing else matches "circuit board / etching / plates with dots and ridges" closely. Do not force a match. The concrete, already-available source material is Tapestry's own height-field library (T-0111: 245 imported presets at `D:\UNITY\Laubrary Dev - Shaper\Assets\Demos\ShaperDemo\TapestryHeightFields\`, plus the live `HeightField`/`TapestrySteel` fill kinds) -- use that as the real input, and separately note in the writeup whether any of the 245 presets already resemble the etched/ridged look described, since that would settle the "do we have the right kind of source material" question without inventing anything new.

**The PM's cost/quality analysis, agreed with the owner, that scopes this task:** the whole Shaper pipeline (fills, effects, caching -- see T-0110-T-0115) already works bake-time/editor-time, never per-frame at runtime. Mesh relief should follow the exact same model: sample a height field per-vertex and displace along the vertex normal, done once at bake/editor time, not as a runtime shader effect. At bake time, cost is close to a non-issue (a few thousand vertices is sub-millisecond work) -- the real constraint is QUALITY, which is bandwidth-limited by vertex density: fine detail (thin lines, sharp ridges) needs the mesh to have enough vertices in the affected area, which likely means a subdivision pass before displacement, not just displacing whatever low-poly primitive already exists. **True boolean/CSG carving (actual cavities with correct silhouette from every angle) is explicitly OUT OF SCOPE for this task** -- it is a materially bigger, riskier undertaking (real CSG libraries, non-manifold-geometry risk) and should only be named as a possible future stretch goal in the writeup, not attempted.

**Details:**
How it works now: open Lathe, add a shape, and there's a new 'Texture Relief' option in its Modifiers list -- pick one of the height-field patterns, and the shape's surface actually pushes in and out to match it, correctly re-lit. This was tested by opening the real tool and watching it deform live, not just by running numbers in the background.

Works well on: box-like shapes with enough surface detail (a plain low-detail box needs more surface detail added first, or the pattern doesn't show up at all -- just looks like a smooth warp).
Does NOT work yet on: round shapes (spheres, cylinders). Tested live and the pattern comes out as chaotic spiky noise instead of relief -- a real, known gap, not hidden.

Other real limitations found on purpose, not glossed over: 1) where two flat sides of a shape meet, pushing each side out on its own can pull a small visible gap open at the seam. 2) if the effect is pushed too far, the surface can fold into itself instead of just getting bumpier.

How to use this: open Lathe, add or select a solid, add the 'Texture Relief' modifier, and assign one of the height-field patterns to its Height Field slot. See the attached report for the full explanation, and the attached pictures for proof -- one shows a height-field pattern that genuinely looks like circuit-board plating, one shows relief failing to read on a too-simple box, one shows it reading correctly with enough detail, one shows the deliberate 'push it too far' failure case, and two show the real editor window with the new option live in it (including the round-shape failure case, for honesty).
